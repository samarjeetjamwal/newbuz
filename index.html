Day 1 — Indian food industry and market opportunities

Goal: understand where value is created and identify the customers you might serve.

Lesson 1: Understand the food value chain

1. Sourcing

Farmers, FPOs, wholesalers, cooperatives and permitted local suppliers.

2. Processing

Sorting, grading, drying, milling, cooking, freezing and bottling.

3. Quality and storage

Hygiene, testing, traceability, suitable packaging and storage controls.

4. Distribution

Retailers, hotels, restaurants, distributors, institutions and online buyers.

Think commercially: a business need not manufacture a product. Aggregation, contract manufacturing, food-service supply, packaging coordination and distribution can also be business models.

LIVE-STYLE CASE 1: The apple surplus

A founder has access to apples in Himachal and wants to purchase a large juice-processing machine immediately.

What should the founder do first?

  1. Record available volume, variety, quality, season and supplier prices.
  2. Ask hotels, shops and beverage buyers what they currently purchase.
  3. Compare grading/packing, outsourced drying, pulp, juice and fresh sales.
  4. Calculate yield, packaging, labour, testing, transport and unsold stock.
  5. Test demand through a lawful, food-safe pilot before purchasing machinery.

Lesson: abundant raw material does not automatically mean a profitable business.

Goal: understand how to develop a product that is safe, consistent, correctly labelled and commercially viable.

Never guess shelf life. Use a qualified food technologist or competent laboratory and an appropriate validated process. Incorrect processing can cause serious food-safety hazards.

Lesson 2: Packaging and labelling

Packaging must protect the product during storage and transport. Consider moisture, oxygen, light, breakage, sealing, food-contact compliance and total pack cost.

This is a preparation checklist, not a complete legal label template. Requirements depend on product and business activity.

Lesson 3: FSSAI and basic compliance

  1. Identify the activity: manufacturing, repacking, storage, transport, retail or food service.
  2. Check current registration/licensing eligibility through the official FoSCoS portal.
  3. Prepare hygienic premises, potable water, cleaning, pest control and staff practices.
  4. Maintain supplier, batch, traceability and complaint records appropriate to the business.
  5. Check other requirements such as Udyam, GST, local trade permissions, Legal Metrology, fire and environmental approvals where applicable.

Do not assume one registration covers every activity. Confirm your exact model before commercial operations.

Lesson 4: Calculate the true cost of a product

Include ingredients, yield loss, packaging, labour, electricity/fuel, testing, freight, commissions, returns, retailer margins and marketing.

Practice example: one snack pack

Net revenue received by the business: ₹100. Variable cost: ₹62. Contribution: ₹38.

Monthly fixed costs: ₹38,000. Simplified break-even = ₹38,000 ÷ ₹38 = 1,000 packs per month.

These figures are illustrative, not a market quotation.

LIVE-STYLE CASE 2: Product complaint

A retailer reports swollen packs and unusual odour. Stop dispatching the affected lot, isolate stock, preserve batch records, investigate the cause with qualified support and follow applicable recall/reporting obligations. Never conceal a complaint or alter a date label.

Day 4 — Project reports, banks, investors and government schemes

Goal: prepare a credible business proposal and understand funding requirements.

Lesson 1: What a Detailed Project Report should contain

  1. Executive summary: Product, customer, location, project cost and funding request.
  2. Promoter profile: Skills, relevant experience, ownership and team.
  3. Market research: Buyer interviews, competitor prices, demand evidence and sales channels.
  4. Technical plan: Process, capacity, premises, machinery, utilities and quality controls.
  5. Project cost: Equipment, installation, fit-out, pre-operative costs, contingency and working capital.
  6. Means of finance: Promoter contribution, proposed loan and any eligible support.
  7. Financial forecasts: Sales, expenses, cash flow, profit and loss, repayment and break-even.
  8. Risk and execution: Supply, seasonality, food safety, credit, insurance and milestones.

Lesson 3: Working capital and cash flow

You may pay for ingredients, packaging and labour before customers pay your invoices. Estimate inventory days, customer credit days, supplier terms and seasonal lows.

Example: You buy ingredients today, hold stock for 20 days and give a retailer 30 days to pay. Cash may remain tied up for roughly 50 days after purchase. Even a profitable invoice can create a cash shortage.

Lesson 4: Presenting to a banker or investor

  1. Explain the customer problem in one sentence.
  2. Show customer interviews, trial sales or genuine buyer interest.
  3. Explain supply, production, quality control and distribution.
  4. Show project costs, unit economics, break-even and cash cycle.
  5. Explain risks and what happens if sales are below expectations.
  6. State the exact funding requirement and how every rupee will be used.

Prepare for questions: Why will customers choose you? What if input costs rise 20%? What if sales are only 60% of forecast? Who buys in the off-season? How will the loan be repaid?

Day 5 — Market research, SWOT and the right business idea

Goal: choose a business that fits your customers, geography, skills and affordable investment.

Lesson 1: Conduct a useful market survey

Interview real buyers rather than relying only on friends and family.

  1. Define one target group, such as 15 hotels or 20 local households.
  2. Ask what they currently buy, how often and from whom.
  3. Record pack sizes, prices, quality problems and buying criteria.
  4. Ask what documents, delivery schedule and payment terms they require.
  5. Offer a lawful, safe product trial and measure paid conversion.

Suggested interview questions

  • Which product do you currently use or sell?
  • What pack size and price move fastest?
  • What problem do you experience with existing suppliers?
  • What quantity do you purchase weekly or monthly?
  • Would you consider a paid trial? What would you need first?

Lesson 2: SWOT analysis

Strengths

Internal advantages: skills, sourcing, network, location.

Weaknesses

Internal limitations: capital, technical skills, distribution.

Opportunities

External openings: buyer gaps, tourism, local supply.

Threats

External risks: seasonality, weather, competitors, regulation.

Turn each weakness and threat into an action. For example, if fruit supply is seasonal, identify alternative suppliers or another product for the off-season.

LIVE-STYLE CASE 4: The small pilot

Instead of investing in a complete factory, a founder validates a product through an appropriately licensed contract processor, where suitable. The founder obtains buyer feedback, records paid orders, calculates actual unit cost and checks repeat purchases before expanding.

Success signals include paid demand, repeat orders, safe and consistent production, reliable supply and positive contribution after channel costs.

Entrepreneur glossary

DPR: Detailed Project Report describing the business, technical setup, market and finances.

Contribution per unit: Net sales per unit minus variable cost per unit.

Break-even: Sales volume at which contribution covers fixed costs in the simplified model.

Working capital: Cash needed to operate while inventory and unpaid invoices tie up funds.

ODOP: One District One Product; check the current applicable district product list and scheme rules.

FPO: Farmer Producer Organisation, potentially useful for aggregating agricultural supply.

SKU: A distinct product or pack variant tracked separately.

DSO: Days Sales Outstanding, an indicator of how long credit sales take to collect.
